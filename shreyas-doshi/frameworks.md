# Shreyas Doshi — Frameworks Reference

Detailed reference for Shreyas's core frameworks. Read this when applying his thinking to complex situations.

## LNO Framework (Full Detail)

**Origin:** Shreyas developed this during his early years as a PM at Google, inspired by Elizabeth Grace Saunders's HBR article on task prioritization. As a self-described perfectionist, he was overwhelmed despite getting promoted — he treated all tasks equally.

**The three categories:**

| Type | Return on Effort | How to Approach | Examples |
|------|-----------------|-----------------|----------|
| **L** (Leverage) | 10x–100x | Perfectionist mode. Best hours. Deep focus. | Product strategy, roadmap, key hiring decisions |
| **N** (Neutral) | ~1.1x | Solid, professional. Nothing over the top. | Partner calls, routine stakeholder updates |
| **O** (Overhead) | Negligible | Actively try to do a "bad" job (you won't). | Most emails, routine status notes, low-stakes bug filing |

**Key insight:** There are very few L tasks on any to-do list. The anxiety comes from L tasks you're avoiding because they're difficult (strategy, hard conversations). "The cave you fear to enter holds the treasure you seek."

**Tactics:**
- Work on L tasks at peak energy (time of day / day of week)
- Choose a new environment for L tasks to build focus associations
- Front-load N and O tasks a couple days before tackling L tasks to go in feeling accomplished

## Good PM vs Great PM (Full 30-Point Framework)

Key contrasts (summarized):

1. Good PMs deliver quality products. Great PMs *singularly improve the company's trajectory*.
2. Metrics-driven vs metrics-*informed* — blending quant and qual by context.
3. Research the domain vs *become the worldwide expert*.
4. Fix UI issues via research vs use diverse methods to inform *what to build*.
5. Logic-only vs logic + understanding that people are driven by emotion.
6. Listen to what customers say vs also listen to what they *don't* say.
7. Inspire with *their* ideas vs facilitate so the *entire team* generates ideas.
8. "Does this make sense?" vs "What am I getting wrong?"
9. Obsess over product details vs obsess over the *entire customer experience* (docs, API, blog, support = also the product).
10. Make convincing arguments for their view vs also argue the *opposite* of their view convincingly.
11. Detailed waterfall PRDs vs *iterative* PRDs that never block eng/design.
12. Work hard, always overwhelmed vs work hard, rarely overwhelmed (understand task leverage).
14. Converge on fixed frameworks vs *adaptive* — wider repertoire, tweaked per team.
15. Advocate for features over infra vs *actively advocate* for infra, tech debt, uptime, security.
16. Answer executives' questions vs *reframe* executives' questions (product reviews are joint truth-seeking).
17. Deal in answers and data vs deal in *questions and wisdom*.
18. Outstanding problem solvers vs outstanding problem *preventers*.
19. Deliver optimal results for a strategy vs first ensure the *strategy itself is optimal*.
20. Describe strategy in an elevator ride vs ensure *their team* can describe it.
21. Give credit to builders vs also give credit to *enablers* (marketing, legal, sales, support).
22. Run post-mortems for themselves vs share lessons with the *broader company*.
23. Weave stakeholder concerns in vs treat stakeholders as *advisors, not approvers*.
24. Espouse company processes vs *edit* company processes — fix unintended flaws.
25. Move heaven and earth for team goals vs sacrifice team goals for *greater company good* when warranted.
26. Study the career ladder vs fixate on *tangible competence and impact* — play the long game.
27. Motivate despite values conflict vs know when to *amicably disembark* from a values-misaligned company.
28. Seek PM-driven companies vs seek *product-obsessed companies* where eng-design-PM-data operate as equals.
29. Learn through work projects vs also learn in personal time from *curiosity and passion for self-improvement*.
30. Attain conventional career success vs also help others around them find *flow and fulfillment*.

**Closing principle:** "Very few PMs are Great. And I don't know any Great PM who does all of the above, all of the time. These ideas should be viewed as signposts, not as commandments."

## Three Levels of Product Work

- **Impact work:** Work that changes the company's trajectory. Strategy, vision, key product decisions.
- **Execution work:** Work that delivers on the plan. Shipping, coordinating, unblocking.
- **Optics work:** Work that makes efforts visible. Updates, presentations, stakeholder management.

**The trap:** Most PMs and teams over-index on execution and optics while under-investing in impact. This feels productive but caps the ceiling. Optimize for impact first.

**Connection to strategy:** When execution feels broken, ask whether it's truly an execution problem or a strategy problem wearing an execution mask.

## Product Thinking vs Project Thinking

| | Project Thinking | Product Thinking |
|--|-----------------|-----------------|
| **Starts with** | Expectations | Motivations |
| **Process** | Plan → resources → coordinate | Conceive → simulate → select |
| **Optimizes for** | Meeting expectations | Creating desired effects |
| **Risk** | Can deliver the wrong thing efficiently | Can be slower but builds the right thing |

**Key insight:** Teams often fail because members are in different modes without realizing it. One person is thinking about *what to build and why* while another is thinking about *how to ship it on time*. Both are necessary — the failure is not recognizing which conversation you're in.

## High Agency

**Definition:** The capacity to take meaningful action and drive outcomes rather than being passive or waiting for permission.

**Characteristics of high-agency people:**
- Strong internal locus of control
- Edit the environment rather than accepting it as given
- Don't wait for perfect conditions to act
- Recognize that "we don't have time to think" is itself the problem — it means you've been reactive for too long

**In product:** Great PMs edit company processes and product ethos *before* espousing them. They identify unintended flaws and fix them rather than working around them.

## Pre-mortems

**Before** a project launches, assume it has already failed. Then ask: "What went wrong?"

**Why it works:** Overcomes optimism bias. People are better at explaining past failures than predicting future ones. The pre-mortem exploits this by simulating a future failure as if it already happened.

**Best practices (from Stripe):**
- Run before major launches or strategic decisions
- Give everyone permission to voice concerns they'd normally suppress
- Document the risks surfaced and assign owners

## Opportunity Cost Thinking

**ROI thinking:** "Is this worth doing?" (Isolated evaluation)
**Opportunity cost thinking:** "Is this the *best* use of our time compared to everything else?" (Comparative evaluation)

ROI thinking leads to full roadmaps of "good" projects. Opportunity cost thinking leads to focused roadmaps of *great* projects.
